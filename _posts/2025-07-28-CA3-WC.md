## Preface

This section addresses the legal and ethical dimensions of working with educational technology, focusing on data protection, transparency, and responsible deployment. My practice is grounded in live-data research and interdisciplinary teaching settings, where compliance with GDPR and data ethics frameworks is not optional but foundational. The following example draws on my role as Principal Investigator for a national research sprint with The Alan Turing Institute, where I was directly responsible for ensuring compliance with the UK Data Protection Act 2018, UK GDPR, and the Institute’s TREx ethical governance framework (Core Value 4).

## Core Area 3a: GDPR and Data Ethics in Digital Education: Insights from Turing DSG

As Principal Investigator for the September 2025 Turing Data Study Group challenge, I hold formal responsibility not only for the scientific direction and educational value of the project, but also for ensuring ethical and legal compliance with the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2018/12/contents" target="_blank" style="text-decoration: underline; color: #004080;">UK Data Protection Act 2018</a> and the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/contents" target="_blank" style="text-decoration: underline; color: #004080;">UK General Data Protection Regulation (UK GDPR)</a>. This includes authoring and clearing the project ethics submission under the Alan Turing Institute’s TREx framework, which is mandatory for all Eligible Research involving the Institute. Every DSG challenge requires a detailed ethics submission (TREx Form 1 and 2), documenting risk mitigation, data provenance, and alignment with both institutional and legal standards. My work is guided by the <a href="https://www.turing.ac.uk/turing-research-ethics-policy-trex-0" target="_blank" style="text-decoration: underline; color: #004080;">The Alan Turing Institute Research Ethics Policy (TREx)</a>, and enforced by <a href="https://www.turing.ac.uk/research-misconduct-policy" target="_blank" style="text-decoration: underline; color: #004080;">The Alan Turing Institute Research Misconduct Policy</a>, ensuring that data handling, risk assessment, and transparency requirements are met throughout the project lifecycle (Core Value 1).

As Principal Investigator, I am solely responsible for ensuring that our project meets the ethical and legal standards required under this framework. The process is rigorous. It begins with a self-assessment (TREx Form 1), followed by a detailed submission (TREx Form 2) that undergoes internal triage, review, and panel evaluation. Each submission must address research purpose, potential harms, data provenance, risk mitigation, and any implications for human participants or sensitive material. Conditions or clarifications may be requested, and approval is contingent on full compliance. The TREx framework aligns closely with the Institute’s Data Protection Assessment Process, and operates alongside its research misconduct protocols, ensuring integrity at every stage of the research lifecycle. The burden of this process is real and necessary. It formalises responsibilities that researchers should already take seriously, and it forces early reflection on potential ethical and practical failings that might otherwise go unaddressed (Core Value 2).

The TREx ethics submission is a rigorous engagement across four principled domains: Fairness, Accountability, Sustainability, and Transparency, each broken into specific obligations. Under Fairness, I had to assess whether model outputs might favour particular sea conditions, wreck types, or geographies, and describe how I would mitigate these structural biases through training data selection and temporal cross-validation. Under Accountability, I was required to document governance structures, version control, and reproducibility mechanisms, from GitHub protocols to shared Overleaf records. Sustainability included both environmental impact and stakeholder positioning, requiring a statement of downstream effects and safeguards against unintended use. Finally, Transparency demanded a plan for public reporting, reproducibility, and the interpretability of outcomes by non-technical stakeholders. Every section required concrete answers about metadata, failure modes, bias sources, and review processes. My assessment of these factors in relation to the use of technologies in this live-data educational reserach, once approved, become auditable commitments (Core Value 4).

***Core Area 3a Reflections***

Before taking on a Principal Investigator role under the Alan Turing Institute’s TREx framework, I had a working understanding of GDPR and a respect for data protection. But TREx reframed that entirely. It didn’t just ask whether our project complied with the law but also demanded a full account of how data would be handled, stored, accessed, and interpreted, from first receipt to final publication (Core Value 1).

This meant confronting issues I had never formalised: whether environmental datasets contained hidden licensing restrictions; whether SAR imagery carried military sensitivity or dual-use implications; whether our machine learning outputs could influence commercial valuations or be used for unintended surveillance. It wasn’t just about what the model would predict, but what someone else might infer from it.

The TREx submission required concrete answers. Who owned the code? Who owned the training data? Could any of it be re-identified? What happened after the study closed? Access had to be locked down, with named collaborators only. All development was sandboxed within the Alan Turing Institute’s Trusted Research Environment (TRE), an ephemeral workspace with no external access, version-tracked commits, and an automatic teardown process that ensures no residual data or credentials persist once the work concludes (Core Value 2).

I had to define which repositories would hold what data, which parts would be made public, and how results would be communicated without misinterpretation. That included publishing metadata protocols, documenting potential biases, and describing mitigation strategies upfront. Archive planning was part of the ethics submission. So was public interpretability. Complete and detailed transparency was requisite (Core Value 4).

This process was new to me, and even though it was time consuming, but incredigly enriching both professionally and personally. It altered how I approach TEL and research teaching. I no longer see educational data tools or student projects as low-stakes or sealed-off. Every dataset carries an origin, an owner, and a future as outputs and algorithms. As an educator, and in this case, as so often is the case, also the student, I realised that when working with data in an educational setting, one is not only teaching skills, but also shaping how future researchers handle responsibility (Core Value 3).

## Core Area 3b: Digital Accessibility in Learning Technology

Accessibility in my teaching is guided by the University of Greenwich’s Principles for Web Content Accessibility, which organise accessible digital practice around the established principles of Perceivable, Operable, Understandable and Robust (POUR). The guidance translates these principles into practical requirements for teaching content: appropriate contrast, meaningful alternative text for images and graphs, subtitles for video, logical document structure, keyboard accessibility, and ensuring that information is not communicated through colour alone. These requirements are particularly relevant to engineering education, where diagrams, plots, mathematical notation, model outputs and specialist software routinely carry information that may be difficult to reproduce in another format (Core Values 1 and 2).

Moodle is the principal digital portal through which students access my modules, and accessibility therefore begins with how material is made available rather than with individual adjustments after a problem has been identified. Course materials are normally posted ahead of teaching, giving students the opportunity to review unfamiliar terminology, diagrams and numerical material before a live session. Lecture recordings are subsequently made available after class, providing a second route through explanations that may have been difficult to follow in real time. Within the University digital environment, accessibility tools also inspect uploaded materials for issues including document structure, headings and alternative descriptions for images. These mechanisms make accessibility part of routine digital course production rather than something considered only when a student declares a particular need (Core Value 2).

The visual character of engineering creates a more specific problem. A hydrogeological cross-section, groundwater contour map, numerical-model output or engineering graph may contain much of its meaning in visual relationships rather than in the surrounding prose. Simply placing such an image online therefore does not necessarily make its information accessible. In lecture material I avoid relying on colour alone to distinguish important information, particularly red highlighting, which may be difficult for some users to distinguish. Where appropriate, colour is supported by labels, annotations, line styles or written explanation. The University guidance similarly requires alternative text for images, charts and graphs, but technical material raises an additional question of quality: a technically present description is of little value if it fails to communicate what is educationally significant about the figure.

This becomes more difficult again when learning depends on specialist digital tools. My teaching uses computational notebooks, MATLAB, modelling software, interactive applications and other technical environments whose interfaces were not necessarily designed around the accessibility requirements of an educational platform. I cannot make every feature of third-party engineering software accessible through changes to my own Moodle material. My response is therefore to avoid allowing a single interface to become the only route to the underlying learning. A numerical or modelling concept may be encountered through a worked example, written explanation, diagram, recorded demonstration and executable computational exercise. The intention is not to remove the technical difficulty of the work, but to reduce the chance that an avoidable feature of its presentation becomes the principal barrier to understanding (Core Value 1).

Accessibility in my teaching also extends beyond disability in the narrow sense. Much of my current teaching is undertaken with an international and multilingual student population, for whom specialist engineering terminology may introduce an additional barrier even where general English proficiency is strong. As discussed elsewhere in this portfolio, I use multilingual resources from the Groundwater Project where appropriate, allowing students to cross-reference technically difficult concepts in another language. This does not replace the formal accessibility requirements expressed in University policy, but it follows the same design principle: where the learning objective is hydrogeological understanding, difficulty created solely by the route through which that understanding is presented should be reduced where reasonably possible.

***Core Area 3b Reflections***

My understanding of accessibility has developed considerably through digital teaching. Earlier in my practice, I tended to think about access primarily in practical terms: whether a student had the material, whether a file opened correctly, whether software could be installed, or whether a recording was available. Those questions still matter, but they are only the beginning. A resource can be technically available while the information within it remains unnecessarily difficult to perceive, navigate or interpret. The distinction between availability and accessibility has therefore become important to how I think about learning technology.

Engineering makes that distinction unusually visible. Much of what I teach is inherently graphical, spatial or computational. A contour plot communicates relationships that are cumbersome to reproduce in prose; a numerical model may only become intuitive once parameters are changed and its response observed; a geological section depends on spatial relationships between units. Accessibility cannot therefore mean converting every learning object into an identical textual equivalent. Instead, I increasingly think in terms of preserving the learning objective while allowing more than one route towards it. The important question is not whether every student encounters an identical representation, but whether an incidental feature of that representation prevents them from engaging with the engineering concept it is intended to teach.

The University’s accessibility principles have also made me more conscious of the difference between procedural compliance and useful accessibility. Alternative text illustrates this particularly well. It is possible to satisfy a technical requirement by attaching a description to an image while still failing to explain the relationship within the image that actually matters to the learner. The same applies to headings, captions and colour choices. Automated checking is valuable because it identifies omissions and structural problems consistently, but it cannot determine whether the resulting material makes educational sense. Professional judgement remains necessary after the automated check has passed.

There are also limits to what I can control. Specialist engineering and scientific software is often developed for professional or research use rather than inclusive education, and some interfaces remain difficult to operate through alternative modes. I cannot redesign those applications, nor would replacing them with artificially simplified equivalents necessarily prepare students for professional practice. What I can control is the educational environment around them. Providing material in advance, recorded demonstrations, supporting explanations, alternative representations and opportunities to revisit the same concept reduces dependence on a single mode of interaction. Multilingual resources serve a similar purpose where language rather than interface design is the principal obstacle.

I therefore regard accessibility less as a separate accommodation added to a completed course and more as a constraint on the original design of digital teaching. Like any engineering constraint, it does not dictate a single solution. It changes the questions that should be asked while the solution is being built: what information is being communicated, what assumptions are being made about how it will be perceived, and whether the technology has introduced a barrier unrelated to the intended intellectual challenge. The objective is not to make engineering easy. It is to ensure, as far as reasonably possible, that the difficulty students encounter is the difficulty of the engineering itself.
